# B2B SaaS research-to-action playbook — fresh run

Run: 04.08.2026  
Research window: 04.08.2025–04.08.2026  
Scope: B2B SaaS positioning, product, growth, marketing, sales, and founder operating practice.  
Evidence rule: the previous `sources.md` was ignored; this result uses the independent [fresh source register](SOURCE_REFRESH.md) and [audit](SOURCE_AUDIT.md).

## Executive decision

Use a narrow, evidence-labelled operating loop: define the buyer problem, test the message in real conversations, collect product evidence, publish one useful point of view, and measure a small behavior change. Do not copy an influencer's tactic merely because the person is prominent. The strongest actionable evidence in this run is first-party practice and bounded case material; the weakest is profile-only LinkedIn discovery, prediction, and self-reported success.

## Recommended operating loop

1. **Choose one painful job and one audience before choosing a channel.** Write the job in the buyer's language, then identify the alternative they use today. This follows Dunford's positioning emphasis and Lenny's job-to-be-done framing; test the wording rather than treating either as a universal formula (source: [April Dunford, “Positioning in the Age of AI,” 21.05.2026](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/positioning-in-the-age-of-ai); [Lenny Rachitsky, “500,000,” 11.09.2023](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/500000)).

2. **Test positioning in live sales or customer conversations before polishing the homepage.** Ask whether the buyer recognizes the problem, what they compare you with, and which outcome they would pay to improve. This is a direct operating preference from Dunford; the measurement choice here is the Playbook's implementation detail (source: [April Dunford, “Post Positioning — Testing and Execution,” 02.04.2026](https://www.positioning.show/?trk=public_post_reshare-text)).

3. **Create one ownable idea from a recurring, named customer problem.** Collect ten verbatim problem descriptions, cluster them, name the pattern only after repetition is visible, and publish the smallest useful framework. Bourgoin's source supports ownable ideas and named problems; the ten-conversation threshold is a reversible test, not her claimed benchmark (source: [Katelyn Bourgoin on Exit Five, “The 6 Types of Ownable Ideas,” 07.04.2026](https://www.exitfive.com/podcast/the-6-types-of-ownable-ideas-and-how-to-find-yours-with-katelyn-bourgoin-founder-of-unignorable)).

4. **Treat AI adoption as a workflow redesign question, not a tool-count contest.** Map one repeatable workflow, define the human approval point, and compare time, error rate, and customer impact against the current process. Wynter's 2026 survey is evidence about its surveyed B2B SaaS marketing directors, while Martell's agent article is personal guidance; neither proves that an AI agent will improve a particular company (source: [Peep Laja/Wynter, “AI in B2B Marketing 2026,” date not captured](https://wynter.com/post/ai-in-b2b-marketing-2026); [Dan Martell, “How to build your first AI agent,” 20.07.2026](https://www.danmartell.com/how-to-build-your-first-ai-agent/)).

5. **Make pricing and packaging answer the buyer's value question before optimizing the checkout flow.** Interview buyers about the outcome, alternative, and budget logic; then test one offer/value-proposition change with a defined conversion or sales-cycle metric. This is a source-derived hypothesis from Wynter, not a promise that “value first” always wins (source: [Peep Laja/Wynter, “8 lessons from optimizing B2B SaaS pricing,” date not captured](https://wynter.com/post/8-lessons-learned-optimizing-b2b-saas-pricing)).

6. **Build distribution around trust and useful evidence, not only reach.** Publish a concrete lesson, show the boundary of the evidence, and invite a knowledgeable peer to challenge it. Fishkin's buyer-journey analysis supports the need to account for fragmented discovery; Bourgoin supports an ownable point of view; the peer-challenge step is an original implementation choice (source: [Rand Fishkin, “Seven marketing problems from pinball-shaped buyer journeys,” 30.11.2025](https://sparktoro.com/blog/the-7-marketing-problems-pinball-shaped-buyer-journeys-create/); [Katelyn Bourgoin on Exit Five, 07.04.2026](https://www.exitfive.com/podcast/the-6-types-of-ownable-ideas-and-how-to-find-yours-with-katelyn-bourgoin-founder-of-unignorable)).

7. **Use a small number of growth bets and keep a written stop rule.** Pick one acquisition or activation experiment, baseline it, run it for a fixed period, and stop if the leading behavior does not move without a quality decline. Lenny's growth framework distinguishes engines, lubricants, and temporary boosts; the fixed stop rule is an analyst implementation choice (source: [Lenny Rachitsky, “All the ways to grow your product,” 23.08.2022](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/all-the-ways-to-grow-your-product)).

8. **Prefer durable founder skills over a long tactic checklist.** Review product understanding, customer conversations, sales, and distribution as capabilities to practice repeatedly. Walling's “four skills” episode and the MicroConf context support this prioritization; the exact weekly review cadence below is a testable operating choice (source: [Rob Walling, “SaaS is hard until you learn these four skills,” 17.06.2026](https://microconfpodcast.com/episodes/microconf-tactics-saas-is-hard-until-you-learn-these-4-skills)).

9. **For enterprise or AI-assisted workflows, keep a human owner for consequential actions.** Require approval before an agent sends a customer-facing commitment, changes pricing, or closes financial operations. SaaStr's examples are editorial/anecdotal and therefore support caution and a test design, not a universal automation claim (source: [SaaStr, “Three humans, 20 AI agents,” 21.07.2026](https://www.saastr.com/3-humans-20-ai-agents-and-yes-we-still-need-real-b2b-software/); [SaaStr, “AI VP of Finance,” 26.07.2026](https://www.saastr.com/our-new-ai-vp-of-finance-closes-the-deal-sends-the-invoice-and-chases-the-cash-it-took-4-deals-to-train-it/)).

10. **Run the loop as a 30-day experiment.** Week 1: interviews and claim ledger. Week 2: message and workflow test. Week 3: publish one evidence-backed asset and ask for disagreement. Week 4: review behavior, quality, and evidence strength. This schedule is a Playbook design, not a source claim; the component recommendations are supported by the citations above and by [Lenny's first-party newsletter practice, 12.03.2026](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-i-built-a-1m-subscriber-newsletter).

## Where experts disagree

### 1. Homepage testing versus live-conversation testing

- **Author A — April Dunford:** prefers testing positioning in live sales conversations before relying on homepage experiments (source: [Positioning Show, 02.04.2026](https://www.positioning.show/?trk=public_post_reshare-text)).
- **Author B — Peep Laja/Wynter:** emphasizes structured buyer research, message testing, and conversion evidence through research and optimization work (source: [Wynter pricing lessons](https://wynter.com/post/8-lessons-learned-optimizing-b2b-saas-pricing)).
- **My choice:** start with live conversations, then test the surviving language in the page or funnel. Conversations expose misunderstanding earlier; page data is still needed before scaling.

### 2. Broad growth menu versus one durable growth engine

- **Author A — Lenny Rachitsky:** separates growth engines from temporary boosts and says a product should find the engine that fits it (source: [All the ways to grow your product](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/all-the-ways-to-grow-your-product)).
- **Author B — Rand Fishkin:** emphasizes fragmented, multi-channel buyer journeys and the limits of single-touch attribution (source: [Pinball-shaped buyer journeys](https://sparktoro.com/blog/the-7-marketing-problems-pinball-shaped-buyer-journeys-create/)).
- **My choice:** choose one primary engine for the experiment, but instrument supporting discovery channels and qualitative mentions. This preserves focus without pretending B2B buying is linear.

### 3. Automation-first versus judgment-first execution

- **Author A — Dan Martell:** teaches building an AI agent to automate a defined workflow (source: [How to build your first AI agent](https://www.danmartell.com/how-to-build-your-first-ai-agent/)).
- **Author B — Rob Walling:** emphasizes taste, judgment, and foundational SaaS skills in the current AI environment (source: [MicroConf Tactics](https://microconfpodcast.com/episodes/microconf-tactics-saas-is-hard-until-you-learn-these-4-skills); [Remote Work Tribe interview](https://www.theremoteworktribe.com/2026/06/11/the-remote-work-tribe-podcast-rob-walling/)).
- **My choice:** automate only bounded, reversible steps with a named human owner. This captures Martell's efficiency upside while respecting Walling's warning that judgment remains a constraint.

## What I rejected and why

1. I rejected **copying an AI-agent workflow as a guaranteed productivity play**. Martell's article is useful operating guidance, but it is first-party advice; Wynter's survey is company-reported and bounded to its sample. There is no evidence here that a particular agent will improve this reader's economics, accuracy, or customer experience. (sources: [Martell](https://www.danmartell.com/how-to-build-your-first-ai-agent/); [Wynter survey](https://wynter.com/post/ai-in-b2b-marketing-2026)).

2. I rejected **treating 2026 marketing predictions as established market facts**. Fishkin explicitly frames the trend article as predictions and self-grades them; predictions can guide hypotheses but cannot justify a categorical recommendation. (source: [Rand Fishkin, “Five big marketing trends for 2026,” 06.01.2026](https://sparktoro.com/blog/the-5-big-trends-that-will-dominate-marketing-in-2026/)).

3. I rejected **using profile-only LinkedIn discovery as expert evidence** for Dave Gerhardt and Devin Reed. The pages establish plausible identity and article leads, but the retrieved material did not preserve enough full text and voice boundaries. They remain candidates in the register. (sources: [Dave Gerhardt profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/davegerhardt); [Devin Reed profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/devinreed)).

## My original ideas

**Evidence-debt budget:** assign every recommendation an evidence class and a “next verification action.” Do not allow a team to carry more than three candidate-level recommendations into a monthly planning meeting; replace one candidate with a verified source before adding another. This was not stated in the collected sources, and it could work because it converts vague research uncertainty into a visible constraint without pretending that more links equal more truth. Test it for two planning cycles by counting unsupported claims found during review.

## Weaknesses of this playbook

- Several candidate rows are profile-level LinkedIn discoveries, not fully captured artifacts; the strongest result should re-collect those pages before quoting them.
- Most sources are expert-owned content, interviews, or company publications. They are useful for hypotheses but are not randomized evidence and may have commercial incentives.
- The 04.08.2025–04.08.2026 window favors recency over long-term replication; older foundational evidence may be omitted.
- The 30-day schedule, ten-interview threshold, three-candidate budget, and human approval gates are analyst-designed and unvalidated.
- No local transcript in the old `youtube-transcript` folder was reliable enough to improve this run; a future run should capture canonical channels, stable video IDs, full transcripts, and timestamps.
- Recommendations about pricing, AI, and attribution may fail for regulated, high-complexity, or very early products. The Playbook does not supply industry-specific legal, security, or financial review.

## Who I would NOT recommend following and why

I would not recommend following **Dave Gerhardt or Devin Reed as primary evidence sources for this run's decisions**, not because the people are necessarily poor practitioners, but because the collected material did not prove enough full-text, dated, attributable content to evaluate their recommendations. They are follow-up candidates, not disqualified people. I would also not recommend following **SaaStr editorial claims blindly**: the source is useful, but its event summaries and case examples are editorial/anecdotal rather than neutral proof. The other named sources also require skepticism; none should be followed uncritically.
